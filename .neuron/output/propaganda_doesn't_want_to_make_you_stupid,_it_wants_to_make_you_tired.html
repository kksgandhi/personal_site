Propaganda doesn’t want to make you stupid, it wants to make you tired

Video transcript:

I am Russian. I grew up in Russia. And when people in the West talk about propaganda, I usually understand the theory they’re describing. But emotionally, it feels foreign because what they imagine is very clean, very cinematic. Someone lies. Someone believes the lie. End of story. That’s comforting because it suggests there is a enemy and a clear victim.

Real propaganda is messier, more boring, more human.

For a long time, I thought propaganda was something that only worked on people who weren’t very smart. I really believed that. I thought I can tell when something is nonsense. I can recognize manipulation. I’m not a child. And that belief itself is exactly what propaganda relies on.

Because propaganda doesn’t need to make you stupid. It just needs to make you tired.

When you live inside it, propaganda doesn’t arrive as an event. There is no moment where you say, “Ah, yes, now the propaganda has begun.” It’s just the part of the environment like humidity, like background noise you don’t consciously listen to, but which still affects how you feel. It’s the TV that’s always on in someone else’s apartment. I remember a specific conversation that killed something in me a little. I was talking to someone I knew casually, not a close friend. We were discussing something we’d both heard on the news. I pointed out very calmly that what he was repeating contradicted what had been said not long ago. I even gave an example. He listened, nodded, and then said almost casually, “Well, you know how it is. Everything is complicated.” And that was it. No argument, no tension, just a smooth exit from reality. I realized in that moment that there was no conversation to be had. Not because he was aggressive or ideological, but because the idea of coherence itself didn’t matter anymore. Consistency wasn’t expected. That’s when propaganda starts doing its real work. Not when you believe it, but when you stop expecting truth at all.

Western discussions often assume propaganda’s goal is loyalty, flags, passion, belief. That happens sometimes, sure, but it’s not necessary. A population that’s disengaged, cynical, and emotionally exhausted is far easier to manage than one that’s angry and curious. Propaganda overwhelms you until your brain quietly decides this topic is too expensive to process properly. I remember mornings when I’d wake up, open my phone, see another heavy headline, and feel a physical resistance. Not fear, not denial, just resistance. Like my body saying, “If you let this in right now, your whole day is gone.” So I’d scroll past it. Not because I forgot, but because I wanted to be able to function.

This is the part people often misunderstand. They think ignoring something means you don’t care. In reality, ignoring is often what caring too much looks like. When you have no power, your life doesn’t pause because the news is horrific. You still need to work. You still need money. You still need to interact with people. Emotional collapse is a luxury most people can’t afford daily. So, propaganda teaches you a habit, not a belief, a habit. Selective attention. You learn over time which topics drain you, which conversations leave you empty, which thoughts spiral too far. And without ever deciding consciously, you start avoiding them. Your finger scrolls faster. Your answers get shorter. your curiosity narrows. Another thing that’s important, propaganda doesn’t need to lie well.

In fact, the clumsier the lie, the better. When lies are obvious, something interesting happens. You stop trusting not just the message, but the entire idea of truth in public space. Words stop being careers of meaning and start being signals, noise, atmosphere. You don’t ask is this true? You ask why are they saying this now? And eventually, even that feels like too much effort.

I remember sitting with my parents while the news was on in the background. It was always on, not because anyone cared deeply, but because silence felt strange. At some point, the presenter said something so absurd that I laughed. I made a sarcastic comment. My father didn’t react at all. He didn’t defend it. He didn’t argue. He just said, “Why are you paying attention to this?” That question hit me harder than any statement. Why was I paying attention? Propaganda teaches you that paying attention is pointless, that caring too much is childish, that trying to build a clear picture of reality is naive. You are sadly trained to believe that the smartest move is distance.

Over time, this changes how you speak. People stop making strong statements. Language becomes indirect, softer, safer. You use jokes instead of clarity. You use irony instead of sincerity. You say you know instead of finishing the thought. You say all this stuff instead of naming anything specific. I noticed this in myself. I start a sentence, realize where it was going and instinctively blur it. Not out of fear in the dramatic sense, out of habit, out of calculation. Why say something clearly if it doesn’t help and might hurt? Clarity becomes something you reserve for very private moments, if at all.

Another myth is that people inside propaganda don’t know it’s fake. Most people I knew understood, at least on some level, that a lot of what they were hearing was nonsense. That didn’t make them immune. Knowing something is false doesn’t protect you if it still shapes the environment you live in. If everyone around you is influenced by the same noise, the same contradictions, the same avoidance, then your personal awareness doesn’t change much. You still have to operate within that system. Propaganda also slowly trains you to doubt yourself, not in a dramatic existential way. In small practical moments, you read something disturbing and immediately feel an urge to soften it, to add context, to say, “Well, maybe it’s not that simple.” Not because you support it, but because strong emotions feel dangerous, excessive, like they will demand action you can’t take. I caught myself doing this and it scared me. I’d read something genuinely horrific. And instead of feeling anger, I’d feel a kind of internal pressure to neutralize it, to balance it, to move on. My brain wasn’t protecting the state. It was protecting me from overload.

Propaganda doesn’t tell you what to think. It teaches you not to feel too much.

Another important thing, propaganda doesn’t isolate you from reality. It overloads you with versions of reality until you stop trusting any of them. The result isn’t blind belief. It’s detachment. Public life starts feeling unreal, unreliable, not worth emotional investment. You retreat into a smaller private world where things make sense. Work, friends, daily routines, personal problems. From the outside, this looks like apathy. From the inside, it feels like survival. One of the strangest parts is how normal everything stays. You can live under constant propaganda and still love genuinely. Still fall in love, still argue about nonsense, still worry about trivial things. Life doesn’t become a tragedy play. It becomes thinner. Some colors fade, some emotions flatten. I remember sitting in a cafe once, scrolling my phone. I read something heavy. I felt that familiar tightness in my chest and then almost immediately I switched to a meme and laughed. The transition was seamless. No guilt, no shock. That scared me more than the news. Propaganda loves that.

Propaganda doesn’t want your outrage. Outrage is unstable. It wants your disengagement, your silence, your ability to live as if nothing is happening, even though something is. Eventually, you stop arguing. Not because you agree, but because arguing feels pointless, almost embarrassing, like trying to reason with a bad weather. You stop saying this is wrong. You start saying, “I don’t know.” You stop saying this is a lie. You start saying, “Who knows what’s really going on.” You don’t defend truth. You retreat from it. That’s how propaganda really works. It doesn’t turn you into a fanatic. It turns you into someone who just wants to get through the day without being emotionally destroyed by things they can’t change. And the most disturbing part is that from the inside this feels reasonable, mature, even calm, balanced. Only later when you leave that environment do you start noticing what it did to you, how careful your language became, how automatically you avoid certain topics, how silence feels normal, even comforting. I noticed it after I left. In conversations where people spoke openly and directly, I felt exposed, almost unsafe. not because they were aggressive, but because clarity itself felt unfamiliar. Some habits don’t disappear quickly.

I’m not saying this to claim moral superiority. I’m not accusing anyone. I’m not saying I handled it better than others. I didn’t. I’m saying this because the western idea of propaganda as simple brainwashing is comforting but wrong. It lets you believe that as long as you are smart, you’re safe. The real version is quieter, more psychological, more human.

Propaganda doesn’t shout at you until you believe. It talks until you’re too tired to care. And that’s why it works.
