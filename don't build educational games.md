There's overwhelming evidence that educational games are effective. If that's the case, why aren't they used more often?

A minor reason is that they often don't "fit" into the structure of most classrooms. The bigger reason is that a complex, large scale, fleshed out capital-V Video Game often isn't worth its cost. For the same amount of money it would take to make one game, you could build a lot of technology-aided educational tooling.

The field of edugames is still valuable though. Instead of trying to make big games work, I think we need to reframe our thinking; not be too boxed into our notion of what an edugame is.

When I started researching educational games, I thought that it had to be an isolated *thing* that someone would play, be educated by, and then they'd move on with their lives. Breaking free of that mold was really helpful. A game can be:

 - A 10 second interactive section in an article.
 - A 10 minute supplement to homework.
 - Part of a facilitated workshop, where the game presents ideas, but the workshop is where the discussion and learning actually happens. A good workshop facilitator can see what happens while people play and then connect their lesson to moments in the game.
 - A way to get students excited, create memorable moments, and introduce concepts (leaving lecture and books to actually push the majority of the content)
 - As simple as a few rules on a sheet of paper.

Heck, you don't even have to build a game to get the benefits of game based learning. You can leverage:

 - Interactivity, by implementing techniques in [[active learning]].
 - Engagement, by using visuals and other media.
 - Competition, through points, badges, and leaderboards.
 - Customized learning, by using software to track student success and deploy specific interventions for students that need them.
 - Realistic scenarios, by giving out projects and [[scaffolding|scaffolded]] real world scenarios.
